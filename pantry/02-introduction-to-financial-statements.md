# Pantry — Chapter 2: Introduction to Financial Statements

## Scenes

- **Roku's September 28, 2017 IPO** — used in §2.1 cold open — anchored to: Form S-1 filed Sept 1; expected $12-14/share raising ~$117M; closed at $23.50. Reusable in any chapter on equity, capital markets, or how disclosed financial statements drive valuation.
- **Chris's Landscaping (sole proprietorship, August 2020)** — used as the running worked example in §2.3 and §2.4 — anchored to: $1,400 revenue, $100 brakes, $50 fuel, $1,000 insurance, $250 net income/ending cash. Reusable for any chapter teaching basic accounting cycle on a small business.
- **Family buying a $200,000 home with $25,000 down and $175,000 mortgage = $25,000 equity** — used in §2.4 to teach equity — extends naturally to appreciation/depreciation scenarios.

## Analogies

- **Financial statements as the "translation that lets strangers price a slice of a company"** — used in §2.1 — explains why disclosure works. *Limit:* breaks down when the disclosed numbers are themselves wrong (Madoff, Enron).
- **Equity as "what's left for the owners after every other claim is settled"** — §2.4. *Limit:* the residual nature is precise on a balance sheet but can be misleading when assets are reported at historical cost rather than market value.

## Etymologies

- **Equity** — from Latin *aequitas* (evenness, fairness). On a balance sheet, equity is what remains *evenly* due to the owners after liabilities are met.
- **Accrual** — from Latin *ad-* (toward) + *crescere* (to grow). Accrual accounting recognizes obligations and earnings as they grow toward becoming cash, not only when cash arrives.

## Trade-offs named

- **Accrual buys better matching of revenues to the period that earned them at the cost of obscuring the cash position.** §2.4. The cash flow statement is the corrective.
- **Sole proprietorship buys ease and pass-through tax at the cost of unlimited liability and limited capital-raising.** §2.5.
- **Corporation buys limited liability and capital access at the cost of double taxation and complexity.** §2.5.

## Scale shifts used

- **Roku's ten thousand independent valuations on a single trading day → one set of disclosed financial statements.** §2.1. Public-market price discovery from a single set of disclosures.
- **A $250 sole proprietorship balance sheet (Chris's Landscaping) → a multi-billion-dollar IPO (Roku).** §2.1 to §2.4. Same four statements scale across orders of magnitude.

## Worked examples

- **Chris's Landscaping income statement, statement of owner's equity, balance sheet for August 31, 2020.** Reusable as the simplest possible complete-set-of-financial-statements example.
- **Cash sale vs credit sale recorded under cash basis vs accrual basis** — $200 sale, $160 supply purchase. §2.4. Reusable in any chapter teaching the two accounting bases.
- **Quality of earnings comparison: Company A (revenue-driven $500K NI) vs Company B (gains-driven $500K NI).** Exercise 9. Reusable in financial-analysis chapters.

## Reusable terms

- **Income Statement, Statement of Owner's Equity, Balance Sheet, Statement of Cash Flows** — the four statements
- **Revenue, Expense, Gain, Loss, Net Income, Net Loss** — income statement elements
- **Asset, Liability, Equity** — balance sheet elements
- **Current vs noncurrent**, **tangible vs intangible** — asset and liability categorizations
- **Sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation** — three legal structures
- **IPO, publicly traded, privately held** — corporate-status terms
- **Cash basis, accrual basis** — two accounting methods
- **Capital, Common Stock, Retained Earnings, Dividends, Withdrawals** — equity-section vocabulary across structures
